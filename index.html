Economics of Generative AI

A tutorial at NeurIPS 2026

Generative AI is rarely deployed as an isolated technology. It arrives as a marketed service, built by developers, sold by providers and used by people whose incentives do not line up. This tutorial introduces the economics of that arrangement, using game theory and mechanism design.

Who the tutorial is for

Researchers working on generative AI who want a clearer view of the economic forces acting on the systems they train and deploy, and researchers in game theory, mechanism design, multi-agent systems and strategic machine learning who want to see which questions this setting opens up. Practitioners working on governance and regulation should find the second and third parts directly useful.

What this tutorial is about

Generative AI has become one of the largest economic undertakings in modern history: more than $700 billion in AI investment is projected for 2026 alone. That capital is a bet not only on what these systems can do, but on the markets forming around them, and on who trains them, who pays for access, who competes, and who is displaced.

Over two hours we follow a model through its economic lifecycle: how training data is priced, how benchmarks create incentives to game them, and how inference is sold. We close with a panel on what all of this does to human work. No background in game theory or economics is assumed.

Programme

Part I 35 minutes Manuel Gomez Rodriguez

Training

Every model is trained on someone's data. We look at market mechanisms for compensating the people who provide it, at the pressure generative models place on human content creation online, and at fine-tuning as a cooperative game between the designers of generalist and domain-specific models.

  • Do data valuations make good data prices?
  • Revenue sharing, targeted degradation, and regulatory intervention on platforms
  • Bargaining and preference aggregation in fine-tuning games
Part II 35 minutes Ander Artola Velasco

Evaluation

A benchmark is less a measurement instrument than a set of incentives. We survey what developers do in response, then turn to mechanisms that make rankings robust to it, to metrics that fold inference cost into the score, and to what win-rate competition does to model diversity.

  • Contamination, sandbagging, and vote rigging as strategic responses to a metric
  • Clone-robust rankings through maximal lotteries
  • Cost-of-pass and other economic evaluation metrics
  • Win-rate competition, homogenisation, and monoculture
Part III 35 minutes Stratis Tsirtsis

Inference

Serving a model is a transaction in which the user cannot see most of what they are paying for. We cover the ways a provider can behave strategically under pay-per-token pricing, what the alternatives reward, and how competition between providers and money from advertisers shape what finally gets served.

  • Tokenization misreporting and model substitution, and how to audit for both
  • Pricing regimes and what each one rewards
  • Adjusting test-time compute to capture demand
  • Ad auctions over generated text, and paying for what an AI overview replaces
Part IV 30 minutes Survey and panel

Generative AI and the future of work

A short survey of the empirical evidence to date on how generative AI affects individual productivity, which is more mixed than the headline numbers suggest, followed by an open discussion with our panelists. Stratis Tsirtsis moderates.

Panel

Five panelists, spanning labour economics, macroeconomic policy, technology regulation, and the study of platforms and markets.

Sandra Wachter

Oxford Internet Institute, University of Oxford

Professor of Technology and Regulation, working on the legal and ethical governance of AI, including algorithmic bias, platform regulation and accountability.

Roland Rathelot

ENSAE, IP Paris and CREST

Professor of Economics, working on labour economics, including job search, hiring, and how AI-based recommendations affect labour-market outcomes.

Marina Tavares

International Monetary Fund

Senior Economist in the Research Department, working on the macroeconomics of automation and AI, including labour-market exposure and its distributional effects.

Ole Teutloff

Oxford Internet Institute and Copenhagen Center for Social Data Science

Postdoctoral researcher using computational social science to study how AI reshapes the skill requirements of jobs, the value of human expertise, and labour-market inequality.

Ana-Andreea Stoica

Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems

Research group leader in the Social Foundations of Computation department, working on algorithms and evaluation for systems that mediate access to information and economic opportunity, including networks, markets and digital labour platforms.
